# Third Back-game of the Second Variable

*On the 11th move of the Black.*

11. **White.** The king's knight, at his bishop's third square.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop's pawn, one move.

12. **White.** The queen's bishop, at his king's third square.
   **Black.** The king's bishop gives check.

13. **White.** The queen's knight, at his bishop's third square.

   > If he takes your knight, his queen's pawn must necessarily fall into your hands, because it cannot be sustained by another pawn; and you may, besides, attack it with all your pieces.

   **Black.** The king's knight, at his king's second square.

14. **White.** The king castles with his rook.
   **Black.** The king's knight, at his bishop's fourth square.

   > He plays this knight to get rid of your bishop, and open afterwards a free passage for his king's bishop upon your king and queen; which would prove very dangerous. -

15. **White.** The queen's rook, at its queen's square.

   > You play this rook, with a view either to oblige him to sustain his queen's pawn, or to take your queen's knight.

   **Black.** The king's knight takes the bishop.

16. **White.** The queen retakes the knight.
   **Black.** The queen's knight, at his king's second square.

17. **White.** The king, at his rook's square.

   > You might have taken his queen's pawn with your knight, and afterwards played your queen to your king's fourth square; but, to force his knight, he would then have disentangled his queen, by giving check: so that this move is absolutely necessary.

   **Black.** The king's bishop takes the knight.

   > As he can no more sustain his queen's pawn, he finds himself under the necessity of taking your knight, to preserve that pawn.

18. **White.** The queen's knight's pawn retakes the bishop.
   **Black.** The king castles.

   > In this situation, the white must win the game: the matter is now, only to hinder the adverse queen's pawn from entering again upon action, and to sustain your centre without being too hasty in advancing your pawns.


---

*A back-game: a variation resuming its parent game at move 11.*

Source: Philidor, *Analysis of the Game of Chess* (London, P. Elmsley, 1777), collated with volume I of the edition of 1790.
