# Gambit of Salvio

*With three back-games and a variable.*

1. **White.** The king's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The same.

2. **White.** The king's bishop's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The king's pawn takes the pawn.

3. **White.** The king's knight, at his bishop's third square.
   **Black.** The king's knight's pawn, two moves.

4. **White.** The king's bishop, at his queen's bishop's fourth square.
   **Black.** The king's knight's pawn, one move.

5. **White.** The king's knight, at the adverse king's fourth square.mmm
   **Black.** The queen gives check.

6. **White.** The king, at his bishop's square.
   **Black.** The king's knight, at his bishop's third square.

   > Doctor Salvio, in his Treatise, printed at Naples, in the year 1723, teaches us this defence of the gambit, but without examining any combination to the bottom. The great number of the moves which naturally arise, and succeed one another at every instant in that party, may very likely have hindered him from analysing and calculating this matter.

7. **White.** The queen, at her king's square.

   > This move may be played in many different ways; but two only can properly be hazarded: the first is, to take the pawn with the bishop, and give check; the other is, to play the queen's pawn two moves. subject of two back-games.

   **Black.** The queen takes the queen.

   > If, instead of taking your queen, he had pushed his king's knight's pawn, you must then have taken his king's bishop's pawn, giving check, and afterwards played your knight to your king's bishop's third square.

8. **White.** The queen takes the queen.
   **Black.** The knight takes the king's pawn.

   > Had he pushed his queen's pawn one move, instead of taking your pawn with his knight, you must have taken his king's bishop's pawn with your knight, and sacrificed your two pieces for two pawns and a rook. The subject of a back-game.

9. **White.** The king's bishop takes the pawn, and gives check.
   **Black.** The king, at his second square

   > If he had played his king to his queen's square, you should have drawn back your king's bishop, in order to give double check with your knight, and make him lose a turn.

10. **White.** The king's bishop, at his queen's knight's third square.

   > In this situation you must win either a pawn, or the change of his king's rook with your knight.

   **Black.** The king's knight, at his bishop's third square.

   > Had he pushed his queen's pawn one move, you must then have attacked his rook with your knight, and afterwards taken his queen's pawn with the same knight, leaving his rook exposed to be taken by your king's bishop.

11. **White.** The queen's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The queen's pawn, one move.

   > You would play very ill in attacking his rook with your knight, who, having no retreat, would be at length forced and taken.

12. **White.** The king's knight, at his queen's third square.
   **Black.** The king's or gambit's pawn, one move.

   > He pushes this pawn, because he is no longer able to sustain it. You must readily take it, because all pawns separated and advanced on your ground must be lost.

13. **White.** The king's knight's pawn takes the pawn.
   **Black.** The pawn retakes the pawn.

14. **White.** The king, at his bishop's second square.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop, at the adverse king's knight's fourth square.

15. **White.** The queen's bishop, at the adverse king's knight's fourth square.
   **Black.** The queen's knight, at his queen's second square.

16. **White.** The queen's knight, at his queen's se cond square.
   **Black.** The queen's rook, at its king's square.

   > In this position the advantage is undoubtedly on the side of the white, because all their pieces are disposed in a better order: nevertheless, this way of playing the gambit is by no means advantageous against a player, to whom a piece should be granted; though this party, played without any odds, is a very good one.
