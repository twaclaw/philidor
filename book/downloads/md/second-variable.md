# Second Variable

*On the 2d move of the Black. (The 1790 edition prints this as the Third Regular Party.)*

1. **White.** The king's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The same.

2. **White.** The king's bishop, at his queen's bishop's fourth square.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop's pawn, one move.

   > He prepares to push his queen's pawn two moves, in order to break the direction of your king's bishop, occupy the centre with his pawns, and retake from you the advantage of the move.

3. **White.** The queen's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The king's pawn takes the pawn.

   > According to his first design, he might have pushed his queen's pawn two steps. This will make the subject of a back-game.

4. **White.** The queen retakes the pawn.
   **Black.** The queen's pawn, one move.

   > If he had played his king's knight to his bishop's third square, you must then have attacked this knight, with your king's pawn; and by such play you would have won many moves.

5. **White.** The king's bishop's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop, at his king's third square.

   > As he cannot break the direction of your king's bishop with his queen's pawn, he opposes his queen's bishop to your king's bishop. If, instead of this move, he had advanced his king's bishop's pawn two steps, you must then have pushed your king's pawn.

6. **White.** The king's bishop takes the bishop.
   **Black.** The king's bishop's pawn retakes the bishop.

7. **White.** The queen's bishop's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The queen's pawn, one move.

   > He makes this move, in order to break your pawns in the centre, and collect there all his strength, on his queen's side. He would have played very ill, if he had pushed upon your queen, his queen's bishop's pawn. The subject of a back-game.

8. **White.** The queen's bishop's pawn takes the pawn.
   **Black.** The king's pawn retakes the pawn.

   > It would be very bad play on his part, to retake with his queen's bishop's pawn, because his pieces would be then shut up again, and besides occupy less ground than yours.

9. **White.** The king's pawn, one move.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop's pawn, one move.

10. **White.** The queen, at her king's bishop's second square.

   > It would be very ill played to give check with your queen, because she is not to be removed from the centre: on the contrary, you must keep her under your pawns, especially in the beginning of a party.

   **Black.** The queen's knight, at his bishop's third square.

11. **White.** The king's knight, at his bishop's third square.
   **Black.** The queen's pawn, one move.

   > It would have been again very bad play, if he had pushed his queen's bishop's pawn; and it will be seen by a back-game on this move, that, with a knot of pawns, it is of great consequence to push forwards the pawn which is at the head of them.

12. **White.** The king castles.
   **Black.** The queen, at her fourth square.

13. **White.** The king's rook, at its queen's square.

   > This move is very material, in order to prevent him from pushing his queen's bishop's pawn, which would bring two of his pawns in a front-line on your ground, and besides give a favorable opportunity to his king's bishop to get out.

   **Black.** The king castles.

   > If he had pushed his queen's knight's pawn two steps, you would have won a pawn by attacking his queen with your queen's knight.

14. **White.** The queen's knight's pawn, one move.
   **Black.** The king's knight, at his rook's third square.

15. **White.** The queen's knight, at his rook's third square.
   **Black.** The king's bishop, at his king's second square.

16. **White.** The queen's bishop, at his queen's knight's second square.

   > This party is quite even; the situation is as good on one side as on the other; the two bishops are mutually entangled, and the rooks entirely free.

Source: Philidor, *Analysis of the Game of Chess* (London, P. Elmsley, 1777), collated with volume I of the edition of 1790.
