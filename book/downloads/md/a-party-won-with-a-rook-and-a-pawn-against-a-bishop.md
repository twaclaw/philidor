# A Party won with a Rook and a Pawn against a Bishop

**Situation of the White**
- The king, at his fourth square.
- The queen's pawn, at his fourth square.
- The rook, at its queen's square.
**Situation of the Black**
- The king, at his third square.
- The king's bishop, at his queen's bishop's second square.

1. **White.** The rook, at its queen's rook's square.

   > If you had pushed your pawn, in giving check, you would have furnished your adversary with the means of making a drawn-game, as will be seen afterwards.

   **Black.** The bishop, at his queen's knight's square.

2. **White.** The rook gives check.
   **Black.** The bishop covers the check.

   > If he had withdrawn his king, you must have pushed yours forward towards your pawn. This party cannot be won, but by advancing your king, and confining that of your adversary in the last line, before you come to push your pawn.

3. **White.** The rook, at the adverse queen's knight's third square.

   > . By this move you put him under a necessity of bringing back his king, and giving place to your own.

   **Black.** The king, at his queen's second square.

4. **White.** The king, at the adverse queen's fourth square.
   **Black.** The bishop, at the adverse king's knight's third square.

5. **White.** The rook gives check, at the adverse queen's knight's second square.
   **Black.** The bishop covers the check.

6. **White.** The rook, at the adverse queen's rook's second square.
   **Black.** The king, at his queen's bishop's square.

7. **White.** The king, at the adverse queen's bishop's third square.

   > In this position the game is won, either by pushing the pawn, or forcing the adverse bishop.

Source: Philidor, *Analysis of the Game of Chess* (London, P. Elmsley, 1777), collated with volume I of the edition of 1790.
