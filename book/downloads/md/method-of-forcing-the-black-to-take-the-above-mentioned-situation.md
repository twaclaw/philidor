# Method of forcing the Black to take the above-mentioned Situation

*In order to give mate with a Rook and Bishop against a Rook.*

**Situation of the White**
- The king, at his fourth square, The bishop, at his king's third square.
- The rook, at its queen's rook's second square.
**Situation of the Black**
- The king, at his third square.
- The rook, at its queen's second square.

1. **White.** The rook, at its queen's knight's second square.

   > If you had given check with your rook, he would have covered with his own; but by this move he is obliged to bring back his rook, which gives you the means of making use of your bishop.

   **Black.** The rook, at its queen's square.

2. **White.** The bishop, at his king's bishop's fourth square.

   > As he cannot cover the check of your rook, you must force his king to retrograde.

   **Black.** The rook, at its king's square.

3. **White.** The rook gives check.
   **Black.** The king, at his bishop's second square.

4. **White.** The king, at the adverse king's bishop's fourth square.
   **Black.** The rook, at the adverse king's second square.

5. **White.** The rook, at its queen's knight's square.

   > This move is very material, in order to have the use of your bishop, as above directed; that is, to hinder him from covering the check, which you will give with your rook,

   **Black.** The rook, at the adverse king's bishop's second square.

6. **White.** The rook gives check.
   **Black.** The king, at his bishop's square.

   > If he had moved the king to his home, you must then have advanced your king to the face of his king, and left your bishop exposed to be taken. In the present situation, the whole difficulty depends upon placing your bishop at the adverse king's fourth square. Your adversary will prevent it, by leaving his king unmoved, and playing his rook, alternately, from the second to the first square of your king's bishop.

7. **White.** The king, at his fourth square.
   **Black.** The king, at his home.

   > If he had given you check with his rook, you must have played your king to the adverse queen's fourth square, and then your bishop would have been free: but, as he could have played his king to his knight's square, we will make it the subject of a back-game.

8. **White.** The bishop, at the adverse king's fourth square.
   **Black.** The rook, at its king's bishop's second square.

9. **White.** The rook gives check.
   **Black.** The king, at his second square.

10. **White.** The king, at the adverse queen's fourth square.
   **Black.** The rook, at the adverse king's bishop's square.

11. **White.** The rook gives check, at the adverse queen's knight's second square.
   **Black.** The king, at his home.

12. **White.** The king, at the adverse king's third square, and finds himself in the position above mentioned.
