2  The editions

Philidor’s book was rewritten twice by its author and translated many times over, and the copies that survive do not all say the same thing. Two lines descend from 1749: one in French, one in English. This transcription follows the English line. A reader comparing a copy from the French line should expect differences, and some are substantial.

2.1 What each edition is

Where a statement here rests on the scans themselves it is marked [scan]; everything else is sourced below. The distinction matters: a title page is evidence, a bookseller’s description is a claim.

1749 — L’Analyze des Échecs, London, in French. Philidor was living in England and published in his own language. Three printings carry the date 1749.1 Its rule on promotion was stricter than what followed: a pawn could be exchanged only for a piece already captured — which the 1777 records in a marginal note about its own earlier edition [scan].

1777 — two books in one year. Analyse du jeu des échecs, nouvelle édition, considérablement augmentée in French,2 and Analysis of the Game of Chess in English. The English one is the base text of this transcription. Its title page reads “A NEW EDITION, GREATLY ENLARGED. By A. D. PHILIDOR … LONDON: Printed for P. ELMSLEY, in the STRAND. MDCCLXXVII” [scan]. It reprints the 1749 material with corrections and adds the supplement, the Gambit of Salvio, the ends of parties, and seventeen rules of the game [scan]. Booksellers describe it as possibly the first chess book published in English;3 that is their claim, not one this project can check.

1790 — Analysis of the Game of Chess, “a new edition, improved and greatly enlarged”, in two volumes. Used here for collation. Its title page adds “TO WHICH IS ADDED, SEVERAL PARTIES, PLAYED BY THE AUTHOR BLINDFOLD, AGAINST THREE ADVERSARIES”, and the imprint is again P. Elmsly in the Strand, M·DCC·XC [scan]. Only volume I is to hand: the copy ends “END OF THE FIRST VOLUME” [scan], and it holds the parties and gambits. Volume II held the ends of parties, which is why chapters 70 to 98 here have never been read against a second source.

1819 — Philidor by way of W. S. Kenny, London, printed for T. and J. Allman. Illustrated by diagrams, on which are marked the situation of the party for the back-games and ends of parties; with critical remarks and notes by the author of the “Stratagems of Chess”. Translated from the last French edition, and further illustrated with notes, by W. S. Kenny.4 Sold by Baldwin, Cradock and Joy, Paternoster Row, and Bell and Bradfute, Edinburgh. Catalogued as the first edition of this translation.5 William Stopford Kenny (1788–1867) had already published Practical Chess Grammar (1817) and Practical Chess Exercises (1818).6 It carries seventeen rules, as the 1777 does — reported by the owner of the copy, not seen here.

The book went on being reprinted long after: something like seventy editions by 1871, and translations into German and Italian.7

2.2 Why a French-line copy differs

The 1819 is not a reprint of the English 1777 or 1790. It comes down the other branch, from a French edition Philidor had gone on revising, and it arrives through a translator who added material of his own. Between the English 1777 and Kenny’s French source lie forty years of the author’s second thoughts and somebody else’s editing.

That is enough to account for what a comparison turns up, and the differences found so far are of three kinds, none of them a fault in either book:

  • The same study, a different verdict. From 3k4/3p4/4r3/3K4/8/1Q6/8/8, the 1777 gives four moves and calls the game drawn; the 1819 heads it “A check mate with a queen against a Castle and a Pawn” and gives eighteen moves ending in mate.
  • The same study, a different board. At the 4th move of the first back-game of the rook and bishop against a rook, the 1819 has the black king on d8 where this text has him on e8.
  • A different arrangement. What is one study in one edition may be a study and a back-game in the other, so the numbering does not line up.

A fourth kind is worth naming separately, because it runs the other way: the 1819 confirmed a reading here. It puts a white queen on b3 in the drawn game against a rook and a pawn, which is where the text says she stands and where this transcription had failed to put her.

Both editions also use “castle” and “rook” for the same piece, which is only a matter of the translator’s English.

2.3 A word on the rules

The 1777 closes with the seventeen rules of the Chess Club in England, and they are not the modern ones. Under that code a stalemated player won the game in England, though not in France; promotion had been restricted to a captured piece in the 1749 edition; and the fifty-move rule was something you claimed rather than something that happened. Where Philidor calls a position won or drawn, that is the code he is judging it under. The repository’s RULES.md sets them out.

The 1819 carries seventeen rules as well, so the code travelled down the French line and through the translation intact in number at least. Whether it survived unchanged in substance is worth knowing, and rule XVI is the one to look at, because an 1819 book sits exactly on the change.

The English rule stood from about 1600 to 1800. The London Chess Club rejected Philidor’s version at its founding in 1807, and Sarratt’s Treatise of 1808 gives the modern one in its rule 24: “If the King be stale-mate, the game is a drawn game.” The draw was widely accepted in England by the early 1820s.8 Kenny’s book falls between the two, so which rule it prints is not predictable — and it decides whether the endgame verdicts in the two editions were reached under the same law at all.
