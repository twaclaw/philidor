# Back-game (Check-mate with a Bishop and a Knight)

*On the 11th move of the Black.*

11. **White.** The knight, at the adverse king's fourth square.
   **Black.** The king, at his queen's square.

12. **White.** The king, at the adverse king's third square.
   **Black.** The king, at his queen's bishop's second square.

13. **White.** The knight, at the adverse queen's second square.

   > It is proper to observe, that the knight does not change the direction of his moves either one way or the other.

   **Black.** The king, at his queen's bishop's third square.

14. **White.** The bishop, at his queen's third square.

   > If you had given check with your bishop, he would have again got his king into the other corner; and this move would have entirely destroyed your attack.

   **Black.** The king, at his queen's bishop's second square.

15. **White.** The bishop, at his king's fourth square.
   **Black.** The king, at his queen's square.

16. **White.** The king, at the adverse queen's third square.
   **Black.** The king, at his home.

17. **White.** The bishop gives check.
   **Black.** The king, at his queen's square.

18. **White.** The king's bishop, at the adverse king's bishop's second square.
   **Black.** The king, at his queen's bishop's square.

19. **White.** The knight, at the adverse queen's bishop's fourth square.

   > It is needless to proceed farther. This position is quite the same as that at the seventeenth move of this game. A PARTY WON WITH A ROOK AND A PAWN AGAINST A BISHOP.


---

*A back-game: a variation resuming its parent game at move 11.*

Source: Philidor, *Analysis of the Game of Chess* (London, P. Elmsley, 1777), collated with volume I of the edition of 1790.
