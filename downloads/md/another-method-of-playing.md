# Another Method of Playing

*(The 1790 edition prints this as the Sixth Regular Party.)*

1. **White.** The king's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop's pawn, two moves.

   > This way of opening the game, when you have not the move, is absolutely defensive, and very far from being the best, especially if some advantage is granted: but it is a very good one to try the strength of an adversary with whose skill you are unacquainted.

2. **White.** The king's bishop's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The queen's knight, at his bishop's third square.

3. **White.** The king's knight, at his bishop's third square.

   > On this move, he might have forced you to play the gambit, by pushing his king's pawn two moves; and you could not have taken, without being exposed to a check from his queen; and he would have won the game.

   **Black.** The king's pawn, one move.

4. **White.** The queen's bishop's pawn, one move.

   > You might have pushed your queen's pawn two moves, in order to change it with his queen's bishop's pawn. This move may be played so, and not badly: but, in that case, you must preserve your queen's bishop, to oppose, in case of need, to his royal bishop, who, placed at his queen's bishop's fourth square, might hinder you from castling on your right side, and be besides very troublesome to all your pieces.

   **Black.** The queen's pawn, one move.

5. **White.** The king's pawn, one move.
   **Black.** The king's bishop's pawn, two moves.

   > Instead of this move, he might have played his queen's pawn one move. In that case, you must have taken his pawn with yours, to hinder him from settling the same position on the side of his queen, who is now standing on your king's side.

6. **White.** The queen's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The king's knight, at his rook's third square.

   > The taking of your queen's bishop, opening the way for the getting out of his queen's knight, would be very bad play on his part; as it would be for you to take his pawn with yours, which would give an advantageous place to his king's bishop.

7. **White.** The queen's bishop, at his king's third square.
   **Black.** The queen, at her knight's third square.

   > He attacks your queen's knight's pawn, with a design to induce you to push it again one move: but it is better to sustain it, because, in case you advance it, he might, by pushing his queen's rook's pawn two moves, form a very successful attack on your left wing.

8. **White.** The queen, at her second square.
   **Black.** The king's knight, at his bishop's second square.

   > Had he attacked your queen's bishop with this knight, you must not then have given him leave to change piece for piece: you should, in that case, have withdrawn your bishop, and afterwards forced his knight to retreat.

9. **White.** The queen's pawn takes the bishop's pawn.

   > As your queen's knight cannot get out, without exposing you to the risk of having either a pawn doubled, or your queen forced, it is necessary to get rid of his king's bishop.

   **Black.** The king's bishop retakes the pawn.

10. **White.** The queen's bishop takes the bishop.
   **Black.** The queen retakes the bishop.

11. **White.** The queen's knight, at his rook's third square.

   > You play this knight, to the end that he may re-unite your royal knight at your queen's fourth square. It is the best place he can take in the present situation. In general, when one has two knights, it is very material to open between them a reciprocal communication, and post them on such squares, where they can be neither attacked by the pawns, nor forced to retreat.

   **Black.** The king's knight's pawn, two moves.

   > He pushes this pawn with a view to break your centre; and it would be very bad play in you to take his pawn.

12. **White.** The king's knight's pawn, one move.
   **Black.** The king's rook's pawn, two moves.

   > He pushes this pawn, with a design to make an opening for his king's rook; and, were you to give him time for pushing it still one move, he could force and break backwards the chain of your pawns.

13. **White.** The king's rook's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The king's knight's pawn takes the bishop's pawn.

   > As your king's knight, being sustained by two pawns, may safely place himself at his fifth square, your adversary takes your pawn, to procure likewise to his king's knight such a post.

14. **White.** The king's knight's pawn retakes the pawn.
   **Black.** The king's rook, at its knight's square.

   > He plays this rook to secure the command of all the line. Here is an example where a rook may well be sacrificed for another piece. I suppose that you don't close that line with your knight; he would then remove to your king's knight's fourth square his rook, which, supported and sustained by two pawns, could not be taken without re-uniting his pawns.

15. **White.** The king's knight, at his fifth square.
   **Black.** The king's knight, at his rook's third square.

   > He plays this knight with a design to procure him as good a place as yours now occupies.

16. **White.** The queen's knight, at his bishop's second square.
   **Black.** The king's knight, at his fifth square.

17. **White.** The queen's knight, at his queen's fourth square.

   > You break by this move the direction of his queen, and your two knights are now re-united to attack and take his king's pawn.

   **Black.** The king, at his second square.

18. **White.** The king's bishop, at his king's second square.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop, at his queen's second square.

19. **White.** The king castles with his rook.

   > It would be very dangerous to castle on your left, because he might very easily form his attack upon your king. In that position, besides, your king must occupy his knight's third square, in order to keep in the adverse king's knight, and in the mean while sustain your pawns.

   **Black.** The queen's rook, at its bishop's square. 20.

20. **White.** The queen's rook, at its bishop's square.

   > This move is absolutely necessary, to hinder him from making himself master of that line, by changing his knight with your's. By this position alone he would undoubtedly win the game. You must always oppose your rooks to the adversary's rooks, and never give up the openings to him. In this situation the game is almost even: nevertheless, as there is a pawn advanced on the ground of the black, some fort of advantage lies on the side of the white.

> **Editorial note.** The editions disagree at Black's 4th move. 1777 reads "the queen's pawn, one move" (d6), as transcribed here; 1790 reads "two moves" (d5), which is also the reading in modern transcriptions of this game and the move the following play assumes. Treat 1777 as a misprint.
