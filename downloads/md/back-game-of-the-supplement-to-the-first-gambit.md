# Back-game of the Supplement to the First Gambit

*On the 6th move of the White.*

6. **White.** The king's knight takes the knight's pawn
   **Black.** The king's knight takes the pawn.

   > He would have played very ill if he had taken your knight with his own.

7. **White.** The queen, at her king's second square.
   **Black.** The same.

   > He is forced to sustain his knight with his queen, lest he should lose him: nevertheless, he might, without danger, have sustained him, by pushing his queen's pawn two moves: in this case, you must have removed your king's knight to his bishop's second square.

8. **White.** The queen's knight, at his bishop's third square.

   > You play this knight to hinder him from pushing his queen's pawn two moves: but if, contrary to your expectation, he should play this same pawn, you may take it without any danger.

   **Black.** The kings knight, at the adverse king's knight's third square.

9. **White.** The queen takes the queen.
   **Black.** The king's bishop retakes the queen.

10. **White.** The king's rook, at its second square.
   **Black.** The queen's pawn, two moves.

   > If he had played the knight to his king's bishop's fourth square, you must then have attacked his king's bishop's with your queen's knight, to get rid of hini; and if he had afterwards taken the rook's pawn, by giving check, you should have taken his bishop with your rook, to give him double check with your queen's knight. In the present condition, both games are almost even: the gambit's pawn must in time be taken, as it is separated, and cannot besides be sustained, but by the pieces.

11. **White.** The king's knight, at the adverse king's fourth square.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop's pawn, one move.

12. **White.** The queen's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The king's knight, at his bishop's fourth square.

13. **White.** The king's knight, at his bishop's third square.
   **Black.** The king's bishop, at his queen's third square.

14. **White.** The king's bishop, at his queen's third square.

   > If you had played your king's bishop to your queen's bishop's fourth square, to attack his king's bishop's pawn, he would then have removed his queen to her king's second square; and your position would not be a good one.


   > If you had taken the pawn with your knight, he would not have taken your royal pawn, giving you check with his queen; but he would have pushed his king's bishop's pawn two moves upon your knight, which you should then have removed to your king's bishop's second square: he would afterwards have taken your royal pawn with his bishop's pawn, and by that means got the advantage of position.

*A back-game: a variation resuming its parent game at move 6.*

Source: Philidor, *Analysis of the Game of Chess* (London, P. Elmsley, 1777), collated with volume I of the edition of 1790.
