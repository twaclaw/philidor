# First Gambit

*With seven back-games; two at the 4th move, one at the 5th, one at the 6th, two at the 7th, and the last at the 8th move.*

1. **White.** The king's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The same.

2. **White.** The king's bishop's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The king's pawn takes the pawn.

3. **White.** The king's knight, at his bishop's third square.
   **Black.** The king's knight's pawn, two moves.

4. **White.** The king's bishop, at his queen's bishop's fourth square.

   > Instead of that move you might have pushed your king's rook's pawn two steps, which would change the game. This will be the subject of a back-game.

   **Black.** The king's bishop, at his knight's second square.

   > If, instead of playing this bishop, he had pushed one step his king's knight's pawn, that move would make another game. A new subject for a back-game.

5. **White.** The king's rook's pawn, two moves.

   > By playing this pawn you oblige him to defend his king's knight's pawn with that of his rook; by which move you keep imprisoned his king's knight, who cannot get out, without leaving his pawns exposed to be taken.-

   **Black.** The king's rook's pawn, one move.

   > He might have made this move in two different manners; the first, by pushing, one step, his king's bishop's pawn; in which case you should have sacrificed your knight, by taking his king's knight's pawn, in order afterwards to give check with your queen, which would ensure you the game; the second, by pushing, one step, his king's knight's pawn upon your knight; another subject of a back-game.

6. **White.** The queen's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The queen's pawn, one move.

   > If, instead of that move, he had advanced, one step, his queen's bishop's pawn, you must have pushed that of your king, in order afterwards to take, by the way, his queen's pawn, in case he had pushed it two steps with a design to obstruct your king's bishop; another subject of a back-game. You are to observe, that in the attack of gambits, the king's bishop is undoubtedly the best piece, and the king's pawn the best pawn.

7. **White.** The queen's bishop's pawn, one move.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop's pawn, one move.

   > If, instead of moving this pawn, he had played his queen's bishop, either to his king's third square, or to your king's knight's fourth square, he might have lost the game in two ways. This is the subject of two back-games on the same moves.

8. **White.** The queen, at her king's second square.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop, at the adverse king's knight's fourth square.

   > He may now, without any danger, play his bishop on this square, as your queen can no more double your king's bishop by attacking his two pawns: but, if, instead of that move, he had played his same bishop to his king's third square, he would have lost the game; the subject of a back-game.

9. **White.** The king's knight's pawn, one move.
   **Black.** The king's pawn takes the pawn.

   > It is very material, in the attack of gambits, not to spare your pawns on the king's side, and even to sacrifice them all, if it be requisite, though merely for the fake of the adversary's king's pawn; because this pawn hinders your queen's bishop from being fit for action, and joining the pieces that form your attack.

10. **White.** The king's rook's pawn takes the pawn.
   **Black.** The rook's pawn retakes the pawn.

11. **White.** The rook takes the rook.
   **Black.** The bishop retakes the rook.

12. **White.** The queen's bishop takes the adverse king's knight's pawn.
   **Black.** The king's bishop, at his third square.

   > If, instead of playing that bishop, he had taken yours with his queen, or had taken your knight with his queen's bishop, he would have lost the game.

13. **White.** The bishop takes the bishop.
   **Black.** The queen takes the bishop.

14. **White.** The queen's knight, at his queen's second square.
   **Black.** The same.

15. **White.** The king castles.
   **Black.** The same.

16. **White.** The rook, at its king's knight's square.
   **Black.** The queen, at the adverse king's bishop's square.

17. **White.** The queen, at her king's knight's second square.
   **Black.** The king's bishop's pawn, two moves

18. **White.** The queen takes the pawn.
   **Black.** The queen takes the queen.

19. **White.** The rook retakes the queen.
   **Black.** The pawn takes the pawn.

20. **White.** The king's bishop takes the knight.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop takes the knight.

21. **White.** The knight takes the bishop.
   **Black.** The pawn retakes the knight.

22. **White.** The bishop, at the adverse king's bishop's second square.
   **Black.** The rook, at its king's bishop's square.

23. **White.** The rook takes the pawn.
   **Black.** The king, at his queen's bishop's second square.

24. **White.** The king, at his queen's second square.

   > If, instead of playing your king, you had pushed your queen's bishop's pawn, you would have lost the game; because your adversary, by pushing his queen's bishop's pawn, would have forced you to take with your queen's pawn, and afterwards attacked your rook and your bishop at once with his knight.

   **Black.** The queen's bishop's pawn, one move.

25. **White.** The bishop, at the adverse king's rook's fourth square.
   **Black.** The rook takes the rook.

26. **White.** The bishop retakes the rook.

   > The bishop having retaken the rook, it is evident that it is a drawn game, except in the case of very great error in play. This game shews, that a gambit equally well attacked and defended is never a decisive party, either on one side, or the other: it is true, that he who gives the pawn, has the pleasure of having always the attack, and the prospect of winning; which would certainly happen, if he who is on the defensive did not play regularly well for the ten or twelve first moves.

Source: Philidor, *Analysis of the Game of Chess* (London, P. Elmsley, 1777), collated with volume I of the edition of 1790.
