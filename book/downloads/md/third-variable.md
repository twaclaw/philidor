# Third Variable

*On the 3d move of the Black. (The 1790 edition prints this as the Fourth Regular Party.)*

1. **White.** The king's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The same.

2. **White.** The king's bishop, at his queen's bishop's fourth square.
   **Black.** The same.

3. **White.** The queen's bishop's pawn, one move.
   **Black.** The king's knight, at his bishop's third square.

4. **White.** The queen's pawn, two moves,

   > You might push this pawn only one move; but then your situation would be the same as that already shewed in the first variable.

   **Black.** The king's pawn takes the pawn.

5. **White.** The queen's bishop's pawn retakes the pawn.

   > Instead of taking, you might push the king's pawn one move. This would be a very good move against a player to whom advantage is granted. The subject of a back game.

   **Black.** The king's bishop gives check.

   > The first part of the former edition proves, that, by the removal of this bishop to his queen's knight's third square, the adversary is set at liberty to establish his pawns in the centre,

6. **White.** The queen's bishop covers the check.
   **Black.** The king's bishop takes the bishop.

7. **White.** The queen's knight retakes the bishop.
   **Black.** The queen's pawn, two moves.

   > Had he not made this move, he would have given you time to occupy the centre with your pawns: the advantage derived from this, is sufficiently proved, as well in the former, as in this edition.

8. **White.** The king's pawn takes the pawn.
   **Black.** The king's knight retakes the pawn.

9. **White.** The queen, at her knight's third square.

   > It is in your power to give check with your queen at your king's second square; but by this move your adversary would be impowered to get out his queen's bishop: besides, the queen and king are never to be placed on the same line, when that is opened and unguarded by the pawns.

   **Black.** The queen's bishop's pawn, one move.

10. **White.** The king's knight, at his king's second square.
   **Black.** The king castles.

11. **White.** The king castles with his rook.
   **Black.** The king's knight, at his queen's knight's third square.

   > He plays this knight with a design, either to get out his queen's bishop, or to get rid of your king's bishop.

12. **White.** The king's bishop, at his queen's third square.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop, at his king's third square.

13. **White.** The queen, at her bishop's second square.
   **Black.** The king's knight's pawn, one move.

14. **White.** The king's bishop's pawn, two moves.

   > You push this pawn, to break the chain of the pawns that cover his king.

   **Black.** The same. 15.1

   > As he has pushed his king's bishop's pawn two moves, you must strive to post one of your knights at the adverse king's fourth square. From that place he cannot be driven out by any piece, unless he be taken, and then you may re-unite your pawns in the cen tre.

15. **White.** The queen's knight, at his king's bishop's third square.
   **Black.** The queen's knight, at his queen's second square.

16. **White.** The queen's knight, at the adverse king's fourth square.
   **Black.** The queen's knight, at his king's bishop's third square.

   > He will not re-unite your pawns by tak ng your knight.

17. **White.** The queen's rook, at its queen's square.
   **Black.** The king's knight, at his queen's fourth square.

18. **White.** The queen, at her second square.

   > This move is necessary, to hinder his knight from taking his place at your king's third square.

   **Black.** The queen's rook's pawn, two moves.

19. **White.** The king's knight, at his queen's bishop's third square.

   > In these reciprocal positions, I should think, the white has the better game, on account of the place where the queen's knight stands: nevertheless, this advantage is not of such consequence, as to decide the game on that side. The black must be mindful, not to take the knight before they have destroyed one of the pawns that sustain the knight, which may be done by changing the queen's bishop's pawn with the adverse queen's pawn; and then both games will be quite even.

Source: Philidor, *Analysis of the Game of Chess* (London, P. Elmsley, 1777), collated with volume I of the edition of 1790.
