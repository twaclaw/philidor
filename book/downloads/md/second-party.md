# Second Party

*With three back-games: the first beginning from the 3d, another from the 8th, and the last from the 26th move.*

1. **White.** The king's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The same.

2. **White.** The king's bishop, at his queen's bishop's fourth square.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop's pawn, one move.

3. **White.** The queen's pawn, two moves.

   > It is absolutely necessary to push this pawn two steps, to hinder him from bringing his pawns into the centre of the chefsboard, which he certainly could do by pushing his queen's pawn two steps, and attacking your bishop, which would get him the move, and the attack upon you.

   **Black.** The pawn takes the pawn.

   > Had he pushed his queen's pawn two steps, instead of taking, that would entirely change the game! So we will make of it the subject of a back-game.

4. **White.** The queen retakes the pawn.
   **Black.** The queen's pawn, one move.

5. **White.** The king's bishop's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop, at his king's third square.

   > He plays this bishop for two good reasons: the first is, to push his queen's pawn, in order to make room for his king's bishop; the second, to oppose it to your king's bishop, and get rid of him in time, according to the rule prescribed in the first game.

6. **White.** The king's bishop, at his queen's third square.
   **Black.** The queen's pawn, one move.

7. **White.** The king's pawn, one move.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop's pawn, one move.

8. **White.** The queen, at her king's bishop's second square.
   **Black.** The queen's knight, at his queen's bishop's third square.

   > If, instead of getting out his pieces, as he does by playing his knight, he should continue to push on his pawns, he might easily lose the game: it must be observed, that one or two pawns, too far advanced, may be reckoned as good as lost, except when all the pieces have an open field to protect them, or when the same pawns may be sustained or supplied by others. This will be demonstrated by a back-game, which will convince you, that two pawns, in a frontline, situated upon the chess-board's fourth square, are better than upor the sixth square.

9. **White.** The queen's bishop's pawn, one move.
   **Black.** The king's knight's pawn, one move.

10. **White.** The king's rook's pawn, one move.
   **Black.** The king's rook's pawn, two moves.

   > He pushes this pawn two steps, to prevent your pawns falling upon his. Here is an observation to be made, that, in the present situation, two equal bodies of pawns are on the chess-board: you have four to three on your king's side, and he has likewise four to three on his queen's side: he that shall be able to separate first his adversary's pawns, on that side where they are the most in number, will undoubtedly win the game.

11. **White.** The king's knight's pawn, one move.

   > This move is most material, because, by pushing his king's rook's pawn one step, he would iminediately cut off the communication between your pawns: your king's knight's pawn could not then join that of your bishop, without being exposed to be taken by his rook's pawn.

   **Black.** The king's knight, at his rook's third square.

12. **White.** the king's knight, at his bishop's third square.
   **Black.** The king's bishop, at his king's second square.

13. **White.** The queen's rook's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The king's knight, at his bishop's fourth square.

14. **White.** The king, at his bishop's square.
   **Black.** The king's rook's pawn, one move.

15. **White.** The king's knight's pawn, one move.
   **Black.** The knight gives check to the king, and to the rook.

16. **White.** The king, at his knight's second square.
   **Black.** The knight takes the rook.

17. **White.** The king takes the knight.

   > Though a rook is commonly a better piece than a knight, nevertheless your game is perhaps in a more advantageous situation than his; because, notwithstanding this loss, your king is safe, and you are the better enabled to form your attack on either side, where your adversary shall chuse to castle.

   **Black.** The queen, at her second square.

18. **White.** The queen, at her king's knight's square.

   > It is of consequence to play your queen, to sustain your king's knight's pawn, for fear he should sacrifice his bishop for your two pawns, which he certainly would do, because all the strength of your game consisting in your pawns, the breaking of them would give him the attack upon you, and probably make you lose the game.

   **Black.** The queen's rook's pawn, two moves.

19. **White.** The queen's bishop, at his king's third square.

   > You play this bishop with an intention to draw your adversary to push his queen's bishop's pawn, which would give you the game very soon, by making an opening for your knights.

   **Black.** The queen's knight's pawn, one move.

20. **White.** The queen's knight, at his rook's third square.
   **Black.** The king castles on his queen's side,

   > He castles on that side to avoid the strength of your pawns upon his king, and the more so, because they are already farther advanced than those on your queen's side.

21. **White.** The king's bishop gives check.
   **Black.** The king, at his queen's bishop's second square.

22. **White.** The queen's knight, at his queen's bishop's second square.

   > Had you given him check with this knight, you would have entangled your bishop, and lost many moves: it is better then to go back.

   **Black.** The queen's rook, at her own home.

23. **White.** The king's bishop, at the adversary's queen's knight's fourth square.
   **Black.** The queen, at her own home,

   > He brings back his queen to her own home, with a design to put her next at her king's bishop's square, and better sustain his queen's bishop's pawn. Civ

24. **White.** The queen's knight's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The queen, at her king's bishop's square.

25. **White.** The queen's knight's pawn takes the adverse queen's bishop's pawn.
   **Black.** The queen's knight's pawn takes the pawn again.

26. **White.** The king's knight, at his queen's second square.

   > You play this knight with a view to pursue your attack on his pawn.

   **Black.** The queen's bishop's pawn, one move.

   > He plays this pawn in order to gain one move, and to hinder your king's knight from placing himself at your queen's knight's third square; but, as this move may be played otherwise, it will be the subject of a backgame.

27. **White.** The king's knight, at his bishop's third square.
   **Black.** The king's bishop's pawn, one move.

   > Whatever piece he may play now, the game is irretrievably lost for him, because your knights have got a free passage into his game.

28. **White.** The queen's bishop gives check.
   **Black.** The king, at his queen's knight's second square.

29. **White.** The bishop takes the knight, and gives check.
   **Black.** The king takes the bishop.

30. **White.** The king's knight gives check.
   **Black.** The king, at his queen's second square.

   > If his king takes your queen's bishop, you have his queen by a discovered check upon him; and if he removes his king elsewhere, he loses his queen's bishop.

31. **White.** The king's bishop's pawn, one move.
   **Black.** The bishop, at his king's knight's square.

32. **White.** The king's pawn gives check.
   **Black.** The king at his home.

33. **White.** The king's knight, at the adversary's queen's knight's fourth square.
   **Black.** The king's bishop, at his queen's third square.

34. **White.** The queen, at her fourth square,

   > The queen takes afterwards the adversary's queen's pawn, puts in danger all his pieces, and wins the game.

   **Black.** lost every where.
