# Back-game (Rook and Pawn against a Rook)

*On the 1st move of the Black, when a Rook and a Pawn win against a Rook.*

1. **White.** The pawn, one move.
   **Black.** The rook, at the adverse queen's rook's square.

2. **White.** The king, at the adverse king's bishop's third square.

   > If you had played your king overagainst his, he could have put himself again in a position to make a drawn-game, by giving check with his rook.

   **Black.** The rook gives check, at the adverse king's bishop's square.

   > Had he given check at his queen's rook's third square, you must have covered that check with your pawn: and if, instead of that move, he had played his king to his queen's square, you must have given check with your rook, and afterwards advanced your king to the second square of his own.

3. **White.** The king, at the adverse king's third square.
   **Black.** The king, at his bishop's square.

   > Had he left his king at the same place, you must have given check, and changed rook for rook.

4. **White.** The rook gives check.
   **Black.** The king, at his knight's second square.

5. **White.** The rook, at the adverse king's square.

   > This is the only move which can insure you the game: every other leads but to a drawn-game.

   **Black.** The rook, at the adverse king's square.

6. **White.** The king, at the adverse queen's second square.
   **Black.** The king, at his bishop's second square.

   > Had he given check with his rook, you must have played your king to his king's second square.

7. **White.** The pawn, one move, giving check.
   **Black.** The king, at his knight's second square.

   > If he had played his king to his bishop's third square, you must then have given check with your rook, and at the next move pushed your pawn. Tiv

8. **White.** The king, at the adverse king's second king, square.

   > Had you pushed your pawn, it would have been a drawn-game.

   **Black.** The rook, at the adverse king's second square.

9. **White.** The rook, at the adverse queen's square.
   **Black.** The rook, at the adverse king's square.

10. **White.** The rook, at its queen's second square.
   **Black.** The rook, at the adverse king's third square.

11. **White.** The rook gives check, at its king's knight's second square.
   **Black.** The king, at his rook's second square.

12. **White.** The king, at the adverse king's bishop's second square.
   **Black.** The rook gives check.

13. **White.** The king, at the adverse king's square.
   **Black.** The rook, at the adverse king's third square.

14. **White.** The pawn, one move.
   **Black.** The rook, at the adverse queen's third

15. **White.** The rook, at its queen's bishop's second square. square.

   > He plays this move, in order to hinder your king from going out at his left hand, and to get him in again under your pawn, by a check, in case you will bring him out at your right hand.

   **Black.** The king, at his knight's second square.

   > In England, where the stale-mate is the loss of the game, you might, at your next move, play your rook to your queen's second square.

16. **White.** The rook, at the adverse queen's bishop's second square.
   **Black.** The rook, at the adverse queen's second square.

17. **White.** The rook, at the adverse queen's second square.
   **Black.** The rook, at the adverse queen's knight's second square.

18. **White.** The rook, at his queen's square.
   **Black.** The rook gives check.

19. **White.** The king, at the adverse queen's second square.
   **Black.** The rook gives check.

20. **White.** The king, at the adverse king's third square.
   **Black.** The rook gives check.

21. **White.** The rook covers the check.
   **Black.** The rook, at its queen's knight's square.

22. **White.** The rook, at the adverse queen's square, and will win.

*A back-game: a variation resuming its parent game at move 1.*

Source: Philidor, *Analysis of the Game of Chess* (London, P. Elmsley, 1777), collated with volume I of the edition of 1790.
