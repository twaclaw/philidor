# First Back-game of the Second Variable

*On the 3d move of the Black.*

3. **White.** The queen's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The same.

4. **White.** The king's pawn takes the pawn.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop's pawn retakes the pawn.

5. **White.** The king's bishop gives check.

   > If, instead of giving check, you had removed this bishop to your queen's third square, you would have lost the advantage of the move, and of the situation besides; because he would have immediately made himself master of the centre, by pushing his king's pawn.

   **Black.** The bishop covers the check.

6. **White.** The king's bishop takes the bishop.
   **Black.** The queen's knight retakes the bishop.

7. **White.** The queen's pawn takes the pawn.
   **Black.** The queen's knight retakes the pawn.

8. **White.** The queen, at her king's second square.
   **Black.** The same.

   > If he had sustained this knight in an other way, he would hade lost him, by your pushing immediately your king's bishop's pawn two moves.

9. **White.** The queen's knight, at his bishop's third square.
   **Black.** The king castles.

10. **White.** The queen's bishop, at his king's bishop's fourth square.
   **Black.** The queen's knight, at his bishop's third square.

11. **White.** The king castles.

   > In this situation, the white must be attentive chiefly to two objects: the first is, to preserve the queen's bishop, who by his direction is very troublesome to the adverse king the other is, to attack the queen's pawn, which, being separated from the other pawns, cannot be more sustained but by the pieces. It is always advantageous to attack a separated pawn, were it only to take up the adversary's pieces.


---

*A back-game: a variation resuming its parent game at move 3.*

Source: Philidor, *Analysis of the Game of Chess* (London, P. Elmsley, 1777), collated with volume I of the edition of 1790.
