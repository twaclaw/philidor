# Third Back-game of the Method of Playing

*On the 4th move of the White.*

4. **White.** The king's knight, at his bishop's third square.
   **Black.** The king's bishop takes the pawn, and gives check.

   > It is by no means disadvantageous to be precluded from castling, when your pieces may easily get out: very often it is even an advantage, provided your king be safe.

5. **White.** The king takes the bishop.
   **Black.** The queen, at her bishop's fourth square, giving check.

6. **White.** The queen's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The queen takes the bishop.

7. **White.** The king's knight takes the adverse pawn.
   **Black.** The queen, at her king's third square.

8. **White.** The queen, at her king's bishop's third square.
   **Black.** The queen's pawn, one move.

9. **White.** The king's knight, at his queen's third square.
   **Black.** The king's knight, at his bishop's third square.

10. **White.** The king's rook, at its king's square.
   **Black.** The king castles.

11. **White.** The queen's bishop, at his king's bishop's fourth square.
   **Black.** The queen's knight's pawn, one move.

12. **White.** The queen's knight, at his queen's second square.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop, at his knight's second square.

   > In that situation, the advantage is on the side of the white, because his pieces are out in better order, and more conveniently disposed.

*A back-game: a variation resuming its parent game at move 4.*

Source: Philidor, *Analysis of the Game of Chess* (London, P. Elmsley, 1777), collated with volume I of the edition of 1790.
