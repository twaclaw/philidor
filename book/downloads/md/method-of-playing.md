# Method of Playing

*Consisting of four variables and five back-games. (The 1790 edition prints this as the First Regular Party.)*

1. **White.** The king's pawn, two moves.
   **Black.** The same.

2. **White.** The king's bishop, at his queen's bishop's fourth square.

   > This is the best place the king's bishop can occupy in the first moves of a party. In that post he batters upon the adverse king's bishop's pawn, against which the first attacks are generally formed.

   **Black.** The same.

   > As the black may play two other different ways, we will hereafter on that account make two different parties, under the name of Variable.

3. **White.** The queen's bishop's pawn, one move.
   **Black.** The queen, at her king's second square.

   > He hinders you from establishing two pawns in a front-line, and occupying the centre. There are, on that move, three other ways of playing for the black; which will be the subject of two variable, and one back-game.

4. **White.** The king's knight, at his bishop's third square.

   > If you had moved this knight to your king's second square, you would have lost the game. The subject of a back-game.

   **Black.** The same.

   > Had he taken your king's bishop's pawn, he would have played very ill. Another subject of a back-game.

5. **White.** The queen, at her king's second square.
   **Black.** The queen's pawn, one move.

6. **White.** The queen's pawn, one move.

   > If you had pushed this pawn two moves, you would have occupied the centre for a moment, but could not have kept it long, as will be seen by a back-game.

   **Black.** The queen's bishop's pawn, one move.

   > If he had moved his queen's bishop to your king's knight's fourth square, you should have pushed immediately your king's rook's pawn one move. You must never leave your pieces restrained by the direction of the bishops, if you can help it.

7. **White.** The king's rook's pawn, one move.
   **Black.** The same.

   > These pawns have been mutually played to hinder the bishops from entangling the knights.

8. **White.** The queen's bishop, at his king's third square.

   > When your queen's pawn cannot break the direction of the adverse king's bishop upon your king's bishop's pawn, you must preserve your queen's bishop, in order to post him at your king's third square; because this bishop is the only piece which can effectually be opposed to the adverse king's bishop.

   **Black.** The king's bishop takes the bishop.

   > If he had moved his king's bishop, instead of taking your bishop, he would have lost a turn: in the exchange of the pieces, it is always best to take the first.

9. **White.** The queen retakes the bishop.
   **Black.** The queen's bishop, at his king's third square.

10. **White.** The king's bishop takes the bishop.
   **Black.** The queen retakes the bishop.

   > You may conceive the advantage of your king's rook's pawn being pushed at the seventh move; for he might now attack you with his king's knight, and afterwards push his king's bishop's pawn, to bring him again into action. These precious moments must be carefully improved, especially in parties wherein your adversary forces out your knights before your pawns.

11. **White.** The queen's knight, at his queen's second square.
   **Black.** The same.

12. **White.** The king castles with his rook.
   **Black.** The same.

   > This game is quite equal; the white have preserved the advantage of the move. He who first puts in motion his king's bishop's pawn, by pushing it two moves, will undoubtedly have the advantage of situation.
