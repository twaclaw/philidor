# Second Back-game (Rook and Bishop against a Rook)

*On the 5th move of the Black.*

5. **White.** The bishop, at his king's knight's third square.
   **Black.** The rook, at the adverse king's bishop's third square.

6. **White.** The bishop, at the adverse queen's third square.
   **Black.** The rook gives check.

7. **White.** The bishop covers the check.
   **Black.** The rook, at the adverse king's bishop's third square.

   > If he had played his king to his bishop's square, you would have played your rook to his king's rook's second square, in order to give mate at the next move.

8. **White.** The rook gives check, at the adverse king's second square.
   **Black.** The king, at his bishop's square.

   > If he had played his king to his queen's square, you must then have played your rook to his queen's knight's second square, in order to give mate at the next move.

9. **White.** The rook, at the adverse queen's bishop's second square.
   **Black.** The king, at his knight's square.

10. **White.** The rook gives check, at the adverse king's knight's second square.
   **Black.** The king, at his bishop's square.

   > If he had played his king to his rook's square, you would have won his rook, giving check by discovery.

11. **White.** The rook, at its king's knight's fourth square.
   **Black.** The king, at his home.

   > If he had played his rook to your king's third square, to prevent the check of your bishop, you must then have played yours to your king's rook's fourth square, in order to give mate at the next move.

12. **White.** The bishop, at his king's bishop's fourth, square, and must win the game.


---

*A back-game: a variation resuming its parent game at move 5.*

Source: Philidor, *Analysis of the Game of Chess* (London, P. Elmsley, 1777), collated with volume I of the edition of 1790.
